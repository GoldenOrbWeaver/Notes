\section{Looking at Data}
Objectives when first looking at data:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Understand the general character of the dataset 
    \item Understand the general behavior of individual parameters
    \item Detect obvious problems with the data
\end{enumerate}

Several steps when analyzing data:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Format conversion \\
    Converting format to something easier to analyze. Format conversion is often needed but can introduce error. 
    \item Reality Checks 
    Check that the data makes sense in context and compared to your expectations.
    \item Time Plots
    Plot the data against time to observe trends.
    \item Histograms
    Plot the distribution of data to observe patterns and potential errors or outliers.
    \item Rate Information
    Plot the rate of change of different parameters.
    \item Scatter Plots
    Plot parameters against each other to observe if there are any correlations.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Probability and Measurement Error}
\begin{definition}
    A \textbf{random variable} is a function whose value is determined by a random event.
\end{definition}

Data with measurement error is an important case of a random variable.

\begin{definition}
    A \textbf{probability} \(P(d)\) is a function describing the probability that 
    random variable \(d\) has a certain value. The total probability of \(d\) over its sample space is 1.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    A \textbf{probability density function} \(p(d)\) is a function whose integral over a subset of the sample space is 
    the probability that \(d\) lies in that section:
    \[
        P(d_1,d_2)=\int_{d_1}^{d_2}p(d)d d
    \]
\end{definition}

Random variables often tend to clump around values. There are three main choices of a typical value: mean, median, and mode.

\begin{definition}
    The \textbf{mode} of a probability density function \(p(d)\) is the point \(d_{mode}\) that has the highest 
    probability density. 
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    The \textbf{median} of a probability density function \(p(d)\) is the value \(d_{median}\) that divides the sample space in half:
    \[
        \int_{d_{min}}^{d_{median}}p(d)dd=\int_{d_{median}}^{d_{max}}p(d)dd
    \]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    The \textbf{mean} or \textbf{expected value} of a probability density function \(p(d)\) is the weighted average of the p.d.f.:
    \begin{align*}
        \overline{d}&\approx \sum_S^M d^{(S)}P(d_s) \\
        \overline{d}&= \int_{d_{min}}^{d_{max}}d p(d) dd
    \end{align*}
\end{definition}

It is also important to measure how tightly the data is clumped around the typical value. This is usually done 
with variance.

\begin{definition}
    The \textbf{variance} of a random variable \(d\)  is:
    \[
        \sigma_d^2=\int_{d_{min}}^{d_{max}}(d-\overline{d} )^2p(d)dd
    \] 

    \(\sigma_d\) is called the \textbf{standard deviation} of \(d\).  
\end{definition}

There are many probability density distributions, but the most important for us are the uniform and normal distributions.

\begin{definition}
    The \textbf{uniform distribution} has the same probability density everywhere in the sample space:
    \[
        p(d)=\frac{1}{d_{max}-d_{min}}
    \]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    The \textbf{normal distribution} is:
    \[
        p(d)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }\sigma }\exp {-\frac{(d-\overline{d} )^2}{2\sigma ^2}}
    \]
\end{definition}

The probability density function of a random variable \(m\) that is a function of a random variable \(d\) can be determined using the chain rule:
\begin{align*}
    P(d_1,d_2)&=\int_{d_1}^{d_2}p(d)dd=\int_{d(m_1)}^{d(m_2)}p[d(m)]\frac{\partial d}{\partial m}dm=\int_{m_1}^{m_2}p(m)dm \\
    \rightarrow p(m)&=p[d(m)]\lvert \frac{\partial d}{\partial m}\rvert
\end{align*}

The mean and variance of a function of a random variable can be found the same way.
